How Commercial IT Works

Business Domains: Retail, Banking, Healthcare & Supply Chain

Technology skills tell you how to build software. Domain knowledge tells you what the software must mean, which rules matter, which failures are unacceptable, and why the business is paying to build it.

Core mental model
Same technology stack ≠ same business system. The domain changes vocabulary, workflows, data, risk, compliance, integrations and success criteria.
Learning objectives

What you should understand after this lesson

The goal is not to become a banking, healthcare, retail or supply-chain expert in one hour. The goal is to learn how to enter an unfamiliar business domain systematically and become useful quickly.

1

Start with the problem

A technically correct system can still be commercially wrong

Imagine an engineer is asked to add a field called Status. That sounds trivial—until you ask what “status” actually means.

RETAIL
Order Status

Created, paid, fulfilling, shipped, delivered, cancelled, returned.

BANKING
Payment Status

Initiated, authorized, pending, settled, reversed, declined.

HEALTHCARE
Encounter Status

Scheduled, checked-in, in-progress, discharged, cancelled.

SUPPLY CHAIN
Shipment Status

Planned, tendered, picked up, in transit, delayed, delivered.

KEY LESSON
The database column may be VARCHAR(30). The business meaning is not.
Every status transition may trigger policies, money movement, inventory changes, notifications, regulatory obligations, downstream integrations or customer-impacting actions.
Core terminology

What do we mean by “business domain”?

A business domain is an area of business activity with its own language, actors, rules, processes, data and measures of success. Software becomes valuable when it models and automates those realities correctly.

Practical method

How an engineer learns a new domain

You do not learn a domain by reading a single requirements document. You build a model progressively from people, process, data, systems and exceptions.

1
Understand the business outcome

What is the organization trying to improve—revenue, safety, throughput, cost, customer experience, risk?

2
Identify actors and responsibilities

Who performs the work? Who approves it? Who owns the data? Who receives the outcome?

3
Map the happy-path process

Draw the normal end-to-end flow in plain business language before discussing classes, APIs or cloud services.

4
Discover exceptions and policies

What can fail? What requires approval? Which transitions are prohibited? What happens after cancellation?

5
Identify data and system-of-record ownership

Which system owns customer, product, account, patient, inventory, shipment or other authoritative records?

6
Learn integrations and timing

What must happen synchronously? What can happen asynchronously? Which partners and external systems participate?

7
Capture non-functional constraints

Security, privacy, audit, resilience, latency, availability, retention, recovery and scale.

8
Validate your model with domain experts

Play the workflow back to product owners, operators and SMEs. Ask them where your model breaks.

Good engineers do not jump from a sentence in a meeting directly into code. They translate business meaning into explicit technical structures and controls.

Look for domain nouns

Customer, Product, Order, OrderLine, Address. These often appear as entities, DTOs, tables and API resources.

Look for domain verbs

Create order, view order, update status, cancel order. These become service methods, endpoints and workflows.

Look for domain rules

What state transitions are allowed? What fields are mandatory? Who can see or change which records?

Look for missing real-world complexity

Taxes, promotions, payment settlement, inventory reservation, returns and fraud may be simplified or absent in a training system.

Industry scenario

One requirement, four domain interpretations

REQUIREMENT
“Allow an authorized user to reverse a completed action.”
This sentence is dangerously incomplete until you know the domain.
Retail

Is this an order cancellation, return, refund, inventory adjustment or payment reversal? Each has different downstream effects.

Banking

Can the transaction actually be reversed, or must a compensating entry be created? Has settlement already occurred?

Healthcare

Can a clinical record be changed, corrected, amended or only superseded? What audit trail must remain?

Supply Chain

Can a shipment be cancelled after pickup? Is rerouting required? What inventory and carrier events have already occurred?

Architectural implication: “Undo” is rarely just a DELETE statement. Commercial systems often require compensating actions, immutable history, approvals and downstream coordination.
Where domain knowledge comes from

Your best documentation may be a person or a production workflow

People

Product managers, operations users, architects, SMEs, customer support, finance, security and compliance teams.

Process

Process maps, SOPs, runbooks, approval flows, incident histories, training guides and manual workarounds.

Systems & Data

Existing screens, APIs, database schemas, event messages, reports, dashboards and integration contracts.

Contracts & Policies

Service obligations, commercial terms, access policies, retention rules and partner agreements.

Regulation & Standards

Applicable laws, industry standards, security controls and internal governance requirements.

Production Evidence

Logs, incidents, support tickets, exception queues and metrics show where the theoretical process differs from reality.

Questions strong engineers ask

Do not start with “Which framework should we use?”

01
What business outcome are we trying to achieve?Revenue, cost, speed, risk reduction, compliance, customer experience?
02
Who are the actors?Humans, organizations, systems, partners and automated processes.
03
What are the authoritative records?Which system owns the truth for each important entity?
04
What transitions are legal?Which state changes are allowed, prohibited, reversible or approval-controlled?
05
What happens when something fails halfway?Retry, rollback, compensate, queue, escalate or require human intervention?
06
What evidence must survive?Audit records, approvals, historical values, timestamps and actor identity.
07
How fresh must the data be?Milliseconds, seconds, minutes, daily batch—or eventually consistent?
08
How will we know the solution is working?Define business and technical KPIs before release.

Common misconceptions

Correct these assumptions early

MYTH
“The business analyst will tell me everything I need.”

Good engineers ask questions, observe workflows and validate assumptions. Domain understanding is a team responsibility.

MYTH
“A CRUD application is a business application.”

CRUD is only mechanics. Commercial value lives in policies, workflows, state transitions, integrations and outcomes.

MYTH
“If two industries use the same word, it means the same thing.”

Words such as order, account, claim, settlement and fulfillment have domain-specific semantics.

MYTH
“Domain knowledge is only for senior people.”

Even junior engineers become more effective when they understand why their code exists and what business rule it enforces.

Lesson recap

Six ideas to carry forward

1
Technology implements a domain.Framework knowledge alone does not define correct behavior.
2
Language matters.Shared vocabulary prevents semantic defects.
3
Workflows matter.State transitions and exceptions reveal real business rules.
4
Risk differs by industry.Money, health, inventory and customer experience create different failure consequences.
5
Systems have ownership boundaries.Know which platform is authoritative for each important record.
6
Domain learning never stops.Production behavior and business change continuously refine your model.
Career perspective

Technical depth + domain depth is a powerful combination

The engineer who understands both the system and the business problem becomes increasingly valuable.

Stage Typical mindset What is developing
01“Tell me what code to write.”Learning syntax, frameworks, team practices and how assigned implementation tasks work.
02“I understand the requirement.”Connecting implementation choices to acceptance criteria and expected business behavior.
03“I understand the process and trade-offs.”Reasoning about workflows, dependencies, risk, exceptions and system boundaries.
04“I can help shape the solution.”Contributing to solution design because technical and domain understanding now reinforce each other.

This progression is not tied to job title. A curious graduate can begin building domain fluency from the first week of employment.
