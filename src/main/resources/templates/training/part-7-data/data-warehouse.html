Data Warehouses

Learn how analytical systems organize trusted historical data for reporting, dashboards and business decision-making.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to

Explain the architecture

Describe the major components, data boundaries, responsibilities and trade-offs using correct commercial terminology.

Reason about production data

Identify data quality, lineage, security, freshness, failure and operational concerns instead of treating analytics as isolated code.

Connect business to data

Translate a business question into sources, transformations, analytical structures and measurable outputs.

Apply it hands-on

Use the Bootcamp e-commerce system as a realistic source and design the next analytical layer around it.

Start with the business problem

Running complex analytical queries directly against operational databases can be slow, disruptive and semantically inconsistent.

Data engineering exists to move from operational records to governed, trustworthy information without losing meaning, lineage or control.

Commercial principle: A data platform is successful when consumers can trust what the data means, where it came from, how fresh it is and who owns it.
Core concepts

Vocabulary you should be able to use correctly

Warehouse
A governed analytical data platform optimized for historical queries, aggregation and reporting.
Fact Table
Stores measurable business events such as sales, shipments, clicks or payments.
Dimension Table
Describes business context such as customer, product, location, supplier, calendar or campaign.
Grain
The exact business meaning of one fact-table row. It must be defined before measures are designed.
Star Schema
A central fact table connected to denormalized dimensions for understandable, efficient analytics.
Slowly Changing Dimension
A technique for retaining changes in descriptive business attributes over time.
End-to-end operating model

Follow the data from source to consumer

1Define the business process

Start with the question: sales, orders, inventory, claims, payments or shipments.

2Declare the grain

State exactly what one row represents before adding measures or dimensions.

3Identify dimensions

Determine the descriptive context used to filter, group and compare measures.

4Identify facts

Choose additive, semi-additive and non-additive measures carefully.

5Load historical data

Maintain keys, effective dates, late-arriving dimensions and change history.

6Build semantic views

Expose consistent business definitions to BI tools and analysts.

7Govern usage

Control access, lineage, freshness, data ownership and certified metrics.

Commercial scenario

Think in business outcomes, not technology names

Executives ask three teams for 'monthly revenue' and receive three different numbers because one uses order date, one uses shipment date and one subtracts returns differently. A warehouse creates governed definitions, historical context and one trusted reporting model.

Business question

What decision, KPI, risk or operational action needs better information?

Data contract

What does each field mean, how fresh must it be, and which system is authoritative?

Operational ownership

Who responds when data is late, incomplete, duplicated, inaccessible or wrong?

Reference application

Connect the lesson to the Bootcamp e-commerce platform

Model a sales star schema from the Bootcamp e-commerce system: FactOrderLine at order-line grain, with Date, Customer, Product and Order Status dimensions. Define measures such as quantity, gross sales and net sales.

Operational source
Spring Boot services, JPA entities and MySQL transaction tables represent the system running the business.
Data platform
Pipelines, storage, transformations, cataloging and analytical compute turn operational records into reusable data products.
Consumers
BI dashboards, analysts, ML models, operational alerts and other applications consume governed outputs.
Governance and security

The data layer inherits enterprise responsibilities

Classification
Know PII, financial, confidential and regulated fields.
Least privilege
Separate ingestion, transformation, administration and consumer access.
Lineage
Trace important outputs back to source systems and transformation logic.
Retention
Keep data only as long as business, legal and recovery needs require.
Common failure modes

What inexperienced teams often get wrong

Undefined grain

Measures become inconsistent because developers cannot state what one row represents.

Fact-to-fact joins

Uncontrolled joins multiply rows and inflate totals.

Dimension history lost

Reports rewrite the past because descriptive changes overwrite prior values.

Warehouse as data dump

Tables accumulate without certified definitions, ownership or usable semantic structure.

Hands-on lab

Practice on a commercial-style problem

Create a paper design for a sales star schema. Define grain, fact table, four dimensions, surrogate keys, three measures and the rule for handling product-category changes over time.

Deliverable: Produce a one-page design showing sources, processing stages, ownership, quality controls, security boundaries, operational evidence and final consumer value.
Knowledge check

Can you reason about the data system?

  1. Which system is authoritative for the business fact and why?
  2. What could make the data technically valid but business-wrong?
  3. How will you detect late, missing, duplicate or malformed data?
  4. Which information is sensitive and who should be able to access it?
  5. How can a consumer trace an output back to its origin?
  6. What happens operationally when this pipeline or data product fails?
Lesson recap

Five things to remember

1. Start with business process and grain.
2. Facts measure events.
3. Dimensions provide context.
4. History is a first-class requirement.
5. A warehouse should create trusted semantics.
Career perspective

Strong engineers understand the data lifecycle, not only SQL or tools

As responsibility grows, you are expected to understand data ownership, business meaning, architecture, quality, security, cost and production behavior across the complete path from source to decision.
